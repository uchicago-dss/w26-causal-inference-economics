\begin{frame}{Background: U.S.--China Trade Relations}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1980}: U.S.\ grants Communist China low tariff rates (MFN status) for the first time, but as a requires annual renewal reflecting Cold War era distrust.
    \item \textbf{1980s}: Renewals are routine. China's share of U.S.\ imports is small.
    \item \textbf{1989}: Tiananmen Square crackdown makes renewal politically contentious. Congress begins using the annual vote as leverage on human rights and Taiwan.
    \item \textbf{1990s}: Legislation to block China's NTR is introduced every year from 1989--1999. Yet U.S.\ imports from China keep growing: from \$51.5B (1996) to \$102B (2001).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\iffalse
  In 1980, the U.S. first gave China low tariff rates, but because China was a communist, state-controlled economy, U.S. law required the president to renew that status every single year
  — this was a Cold War–era provision designed for nonmarket economies like the Soviet Union and China.
  Through the 1980s, these renewals were basically automatic. But after Tiananmen Square in 1989,
  Congress started using the annual vote to pressure China on human rights, Taiwan, and arms sales.
  Every year from 1989 to 1999, someone tried to block renewal.
  And yet, during this same period, U.S. imports from China nearly doubled. 
  So you had this growing tension: China is becoming one of our biggest trade partners, 
  but its access to low tariffs depends on a contested political vote every year. 
  That's the uncertainty at the heart of our study."
\fi

\begin{frame}{Policy Timeline}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-2001}: China receives low tariff rates (NTR) each year, but Congress must vote to renew. If renewal fails, tariffs revert to Smoot-Hawley rates from 1930 (an average jump from 4\% to 37\%).
    \item \textbf{October 2000}: Congress passes the U.S.--China Relations Act, granting China \textbf{Permanent Normal Trade Relations (PNTR)}, conditional on WTO accession.
    \item \textbf{December 2001}: China officially joins the WTO.
    \item \textbf{January 2002}: Permanent low tariffs take effect. The annual renewal process is eliminated.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  \begin{block}{Critical insight}
    The actual tariff rates mostly \textbf{did not change}: China was already receiving the low NTR rate every year. What changed was the \textbf{permanent elimination of uncertainty} about whether those low rates would continue.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}
\iffalse
  Here's the timeline of how that uncertainty got resolved.
  Before 2001, if Congress didn't renew China's low tariff status, 
  tariffs would have automatically reverted to rates from the 1930s Smoot-Hawley Act
    — that's an average jump from about 4 percent to 37 percent. That's enormous.
  In October 2000, Congress passed the U.S.–China Relations Act granting permanent normal trade relations, 
  but it was conditional on China actually joining the WTO. China joined in December 2001, 
  and permanent low tariffs took effect January 2002.
  The critical insight — and this is what drives our entire research design
  — is that the actual tariff rates mostly did not change. 
  China was already getting the low rate every year. 
  What changed was that the uncertainty disappeared permanently.
    And that uncertainty affected some products much more than others, 
    which is what we exploit in our identification strategy.
\fi

    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification Intuition}
  \vspace{0.3cm}
  \textbf{Why did uncertainty matter differently across products?}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Every product had two legislated tariff rates: a low NTR rate and a high non-NTR rate.
    \item The \textbf{NTR gap} = non-NTR rate $-$ NTR rate measures how much a product's tariff \emph{would have jumped} if Congress didn't renew.
    \item Products with large gaps faced more risk $\rightarrow$ importers could be heavily impacted.
    \item Products with small gaps faced less risk $\rightarrow$ less impact by the annual vote.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\iffalse
  Speaker notes: Before 2001, Congress renewed China's low tariffs annually.
  In 2000, PNTR was granted. In January 2002, WTO accession made it permanent, so now the low tariffs were guaranteed to continue indefinitely.
  Now, the uncertainty wasn't equal across products. Every product has two tariff rates written into law —
  the low NTR rate and the high non-NTR rate from 1930. The gap between these rates tells us how exposed each product was.
  A product with a 45 percentage point gap faced serious risk
  — importers might hold back on long-term contracts. A product with a 3 percentage point gap had almost nothing to worry about. When the uncertainty was removed, we'd expect the high-gap products to respond the most."
\fi

\begin{frame}{Identification Strategy: Difference-in-Differences}
  \small
  \begin{block}{Core idea}
    Compare import growth for \textbf{high-gap} products (more exposed to uncertainty) vs.\ \textbf{low-gap} products (less exposed), before and after WTO entry.
  \end{block}

  % \vspace{0.2cm}
  % \textbf{Why this works:}
  % \begin{itemize}
  %   \item Low-gap products serve as a \textbf{control group}: because, while they were still affected, they had a small stake from the policy change.
  %   \item Any ``extra'' import growth in high-gap products after 2002, beyond what low-gap products experienced, can be attributed to the removal of tariff uncertainty.
  % \end{itemize}


  % edit this slide te more explicit about the treatment and control groups and that was assume that only difference between the treatment and control group (after our controls) is the policy change
  \vspace{0.2cm}
  \textbf{Treatment exposure (measured in 1999, before WTO negotiations):}
  \begin{equation*}
      \text{gap\_pre}_p = (\text{non-NTR tariff})_p - (\text{NTR tariff})_p
  \end{equation*}

  Products above the median gap $\rightarrow$ ``high-gap'' (treatment); below $\rightarrow$ ``low-gap'' (control).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Baseline Specification}
  \small
  \begin{block}{Two-way FE DiD}
    \begin{equation*}
      Y_{pt} = \beta\,(\text{HighGap}_p \times \text{Post}_t) + \delta_p + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{pt}
    \end{equation*}
  \end{block}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item $Y_{pt}$: log import value, log quantity, or log unit value.
    \item $\delta_p$: product fixed effects.
    \item $\lambda_t$: time fixed effects.
    \item SEs clustered at product level.
  \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Main DiD Results (Current Outputs)}
  \small
  \textbf{Binary treatment (\texttt{HighGap x Post}) with product and date FE}
  \begin{itemize}

    \item \texttt{ln\_first\it\_qty}: $\hat\beta \approx 0.145$ (s.e. 0.036), positive and significant.
